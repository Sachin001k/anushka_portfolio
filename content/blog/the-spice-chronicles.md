Walk into any home in India, and you are bound to catch the unmistakable aroma of spices. From the comforting crackle of cumin in hot oil to the warm scent of cardamom rising from a cup of chai, spices aren’t just ingredients in our food, but now they hold stories, traditions, and a dash of magic, our mothers swear by. As a science student, the way I look at spices has evolved over the years. I don't think of them just as flavours, but as tiny packets of culture, chemistry, and comfort.

![](/assets/images/blog/the-spice-chronicles.webp)

## More Than Just Taste

We often associate spices with flavour, and rightfully so, as our food wouldn’t be the same without them. However, long before we had refrigerators or air-tight containers, spices played a different role. Clove, cinnamon, and mustard seeds all have natural properties that have helped preserve food and keep it edible in our hot and humid climate. That little tadka our mothers add to dal? It wasn’t just for taste; it helped make the food last longer, too. So every time we see someone add a bay leaf to rice, or mustard seeds to a pickle, it is a practice passed down through generations. Something that hasn’t been created in science labs but in kitchens in our homes.

## Rituals We Didn’t Know Were Rituals

Turmeric isn’t just a spice in our masala dabba but a part of weddings, pujas, and school projects (who else remembers painting with haldi and water?). Every spice in our kitchen seems to carry a role beyond cooking. In many homes, a drawer full of dried red chillies and bay leaves isn’t just about recipes, but it’s part of seasonal rituals like stocking up before monsoon or sun-drying masalas in March before summer hits full swing.

What stands out to me is how naturally spices have become part of our everyday routines. No one pauses to measure out jeera or mustard seeds — you just add what feels right. Some steps, like tossing in curry leaves after the oil heats up, happen almost without thinking. Even in kitchens filled with gadgets and ready-to-eat packs, the basic spice process hasn’t changed much. It’s just something we all pick up, whether by watching our parents cook or figuring it out ourselves.

## Spices And Small Wonders

One of my favourite things about spices is how they sneak in a little science. Take turmeric, did you know it can change colour when it meets something alkaline like soap or baking soda? It turns from yellow to red! Or how chilli tricks your brain into thinking your mouth is on fire, even though there’s no heat at all?

Even something as common as mustard seeds behaves like a science experiment. Quiet one second, then suddenly popping and dancing in the oil the next. Cooking Indian food is full of these little reactions that make you feel like you’re conducting experiments (except the results are edible).

## A Global Love Affair That Started Here

It’s also worth remembering that Indian spices didn’t just stay in India. People from across the world once travelled here for our spices. Black pepper was called “black gold” and traded like currency. Saffron, cardamom, and cinnamon were our exports before software and steel. And while those explorers may have come for the spices, they left with stories, techniques, and even parts of our culture.

## In Every Spoon, There Is A Story

Living in India, it’s easy to think of spices as routine. We smell them every day, and we use them without thinking. But the more I learn about them, the more I realise: they’re not just “kitchen stuff.” They’re history, culture, memory, and yes, a bit of everyday science too.

So the next time you stir a curry or sip masala chai, pause for a second. That cinnamon stick or clove isn’t just adding taste — it’s carrying a piece of who we are, and where we come from.
